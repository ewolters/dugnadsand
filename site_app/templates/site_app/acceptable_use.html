Acceptable use

Applies to dugnadsand.org and to every organization admitted to it

This states what the network is for, what it is not for, and what happens when somebody uses it for the second thing.

How this differs from the operating policy

The statement of operating policy lists eleven commitments, each bound to a check that runs against the source and reports at the attestation. Nothing on this page works that way. There is no executable check for whether a posting is campaigning or whether a message is abusive, and a commitment with nothing behind it would be worse than no commitment: it would read as enforcement while enforcing nothing.

What follows is therefore a standard applied by people, and the section on enforcement says exactly which mechanisms exist and which do not.

1. What the network is for

Practical mutual aid within a chapter: work offered, work needed, material that exists and can be moved, and the coordination of both. A posting describes something somebody can do or something somebody needs done.

Every organization in a chapter can see every other organization's postings and take up any of them. That visibility is the point, and it is also why the standard below exists: a shared board carries whatever is put on it.

2. Political activity

The network is not a channel for electoral politics. The following do not belong on it:

  • Support for or opposition to a candidate for public office, a party, or a slate.
  • Advocacy for or against a ballot measure, referendum, or piece of legislation.
  • Recruitment to a campaign, canvass, protest, or political organization, whether or not framed as a request for volunteers.
  • Fundraising or signature collection for any of the above.

This is narrower than a rule against holding or expressing views, which would be neither enforceable nor desirable. People in a chapter will disagree about a great deal. The restriction is on using a shared coordination board as a campaign channel, and the test is what a posting asks somebody to do.

The restriction has a second basis for some members. Not-for-profit organizations admitted to the network are subject to limits on political campaign intervention under their own tax status. A network that carried campaign activity would place that at risk for organizations that never chose it.

3. Commercial use

Nothing in the network is bought or sold, and no posting may quote a price, solicit payment, or route to a channel where either happens. Businesses are admitted as parties — an electrician's licence is checked at the door — and they participate on the same terms as anybody else.

Advertising, lead generation, and collecting contact details for use outside the network are all outside what the network is for.

4. Conduct

Members meet in person, work at each other's addresses, and hand each other tools. The standard follows from that rather than from a general theory of speech:

  • Harassment of a member, including repeated unwanted contact after being asked to stop.
  • Threats, intimidation, or abuse directed at a person.
  • Attacks on a person's race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, disability, sex, sexual orientation, or gender identity.
  • Publishing another member's address, telephone number, or other personal details beyond what they put on the board themselves.
  • Misrepresenting who is asking, or what is being asked for.

5. Safety

The network coordinates work and does not supervise it. It carries no insurance for any member, verifies no competence, and makes no assessment of whether a task is safe to attempt.

Admission is registration, not endorsement. An organization tells the network what it holds — a licence, a certificate of insurance, a tax number — and that statement is recorded as made. The network does not confirm it, does not vouch for it, and being admitted here is not a representation by the network that any of it is true. An officer may look at a document before deciding and may record that they did; that is one person's note on one day, and it is not published to anybody.

Work involving minors, work in somebody's home, and work with tools or vehicles remain the responsibility of the people doing it and of the organizations they belong to.

Licensed trades

An organization admitted with a licence to practise offers work under that licence. A licence is not suspended because work is unpaid, and an insurer does not treat a panel rewired as a favour differently from one rewired for a fee. Where a licence is on file, the board asks for that affirmation each time an offer is posted or a member puts their name to somebody else's, and records the affirmation against the offer.

What is recorded is that the affirmation was made and which licence was named. Nothing confirms the licence exists, that it is current, or that the work falls within its scope. Those are things the licence holder knows and the network does not, so the affirmation asks them to say so rather than checking a date on file. Scope, competence and currency are matters between the licence holder and whoever issued it.

Requests for help

A request submitted at need help is shown to the mutual aid groups covering that area and to no other member. What follows is between the group that takes it up and the person who asked. The network records that a request was taken up and that it closed. It records nothing about what was done, whether it helped, or how it went, and it is not a party to any of it.

6. How this is enforced

Precisely, and less than a document of this kind usually implies. The mechanisms that exist today are these, in the order they are reached:

A person speaks to a person.
The first and most common remedy involves no software at all. It depends on chapters staying small and local enough for the people in one to reach each other.
The author takes it down.
A posting can be removed by the member who wrote it and by nobody else. There is no moderator account, and no officer can delete another member's posting or comment. This is a real limit, stated rather than glossed.
An application is declined at the door.
Admission is where the network exercises the most control. A chapter officer reviews every application, checks credentials against whoever issued them, and may decline without giving a reason.
An organization is removed from its chapter.
The strongest remedy available, and the only one an officer can carry out against somebody else's organization. An organization removed from a chapter becomes invisible to every member of it — enforced by the database rather than by a setting somebody has to remember — and its members can no longer see or take up anything in it. A reason is required and is kept with the record of who removed it. Nothing the organization wrote is deleted and its members keep their logins: it is scoped to itself, as an organization admitted into no chapter has always been. Removal from a room is not erasure from the record, and the two are not reachable by the same control.

There is no automated detection of any kind, no reporting queue, and no record of warnings. Nothing counts how often a member has been the subject of a complaint, because such a count is a score and the system holds none.

7. Raising something

Concerns go to a chapter officer, or through the contact form where a chapter officer cannot be reached or is the subject of the concern. Both reach a person, and neither creates a ticket.

8. Changes

This page is not versioned against the manifest, because it makes no claim the manifest can check. It is changed by the people who run the network, and an admitted organization that disagrees with a change may leave — the record it wrote stays with it, and nothing in the network is owed to anybody.
