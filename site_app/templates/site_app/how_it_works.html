How it works

The front page is the idea. This is the machinery — what gets written down, what deliberately never does, and how to check that for yourself rather than taking our word for it.

Four things people share

Hours

Somebody does something and it gets written down. Thirteen hours on a community garden. That is the entire entry.

What's offered, and what's needed

A board that runs both directions. Put up what you have, put up what you need, and anyone can take up either. Nobody has to have given first, and asking costs nothing and proves nothing.

Ongoing work

A project gathers related postings — repairing homes on the east side, keeping the pantry stocked. Nobody is put in charge of one. Anyone can mark a project finished, including somebody who never worked on it, because otherwise the first person to move away strands whatever they wrote down.

Material

The part most systems leave out. It has a section of its own below.

What the record is not

It is not a currency.

Hours cannot be spent, are not owed by anyone to anyone, and buy nothing. Nothing in the system decides anything based on what you have given. You can receive having given nothing, ever, and nobody can see whether you have.

That one property is the whole design. Everything else is shaped to protect it.

There is no total anywhere — not per person, not per project, not your own. A number that can be compared between people is a score, and a score brings back through social pressure exactly what the code removes.

You can also stop whenever. Taking something on and then stepping away deletes the record of it: not marked withdrawn, not counted, gone. A stored record of stopping is a record of not following through, and anything stored can eventually be counted by somebody.

The virtual warehouse

Willing hands are rarely the bottleneck. A pallet of shingles is.

A farm or a business lists a place they keep material, and what is in it. The goods never enter our custody. They stay where their holder keeps them; this stores a location, a description and a count. That keeps this a directory rather than a warehouse, which matters legally as much as practically — and it is the same shape that keeps the hours a record rather than a currency.

We can prove a thing moved. We never say what it was worth.

Every amount shows how recently the person holding it last looked — confirmed today, not confirmed in four weeks. Only the holder can move that clock, and shipping something out deliberately does not: the sender knows what they sent, they have not re-counted what is left. Listings that have gone quiet are dimmed rather than hidden, because the material may well still be there.

When material moves it produces a manifest with a QR code. Whoever takes delivery scans it to sign for it — no account, no sign-up, because they are standing in a yard with a phone, and a receipt nobody can record is a receipt that never happens.

Bills of material

A project can say what it needs, not just who it needs.

Reclaimed oak, two hundred board-feet. People record what actually arrives against it, and material can come straight from a warehouse listing so the paperwork and the project agree.

Hours and material are kept in separate lists and are never added together. There is no way to record what material is worth, and no way to say what it is worth in hours. Both would put a price on a gift, and a priced gift is a transaction.

The one figure the system does show is what is still needed — eighty board-feet short. That is a fact about the project rather than about any person, and material cannot be coordinated without it.

Your organization is your own

Each organization is admitted deliberately, and its members, postings, ledger and material are invisible to every other one. That separation is enforced by the database itself rather than by application code remembering to filter, and it fails closed: a mistake makes rows disappear rather than leak.

Anything that leaves the system — a notification, an email, an integration — carries only that something exists, and a link. Never the words somebody wrote, never a member's name. Those stay inside and are fetched when the reader signs in.

How to check any of this

Everything above is a claim, and claims are cheap.

So each one is tied to a test that runs continuously, and the results are published at /attestation/ — run live when you load that page, so a stalled checker cannot make a green record look like a green system.

ClaimWhat it means
no-gatingNo code path consults what a member has given when deciding what they may receive
no-balanceNo record carries a balance, a credit limit, or a spendable total
no-material-valuationNo record of material carries a value, a price, or an equivalence in hours
no-obligationOffers are ceilings. Nothing records a minimum, a completion, or a penalty for stopping
no-aggregate-displayNo per-member total is computed or displayed

A check whose subject does not exist yet reports incomplete rather than passing, and a run can never read green while any check is in that state. An honest amber is the right answer until the thing is built.

Not a legal attestation This says what the code was checked for and what the checks returned. It is not legal advice, it is not a tax opinion, and it does not tell your organization what its obligations are — those are questions for your own counsel and accountant. What it can tell you is exactly what the software does and does not do, which is the part we can be held to.

The code is public at github.com/ewolters/dugnadsand. The claims live in policy/manifest.toml, the checks behind them in policy/checks.py, and the reasoning in docs/design-rules.md.
